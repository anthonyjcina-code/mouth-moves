    For grown-ups

    Mouth Moves is practice for a child with autism who is learning to speak. It works on the speech problems that are common in autism, one at a time. It does not replace a speech-language pathologist (SLP).

    What it helps with

    Copying mouth movements is hard
    You see inside the mouth from the side. Each sound moves in two or three small steps, and your child taps for the next one. He sets the pace.
    Planning the movements for speech is hard (apraxia of speech)
    Sounds are split into small moves and repeated in short rounds. Sounds you can see on the lips come first (m, p, b). Sounds made behind the teeth come after.
    Faces and eye contact feel uncomfortable
    The cartoon head has a closed eye and never asks to be looked at. He learns the sound from the mouth picture.
    Few words for asking
    The I want board pairs a word with a real thing. After a try, you give a small bit right away, so speaking works for him.
    Echoing, or needing extra time to answer
    The app shows the sound once, then waits quietly. You choose 3 to 10 seconds. Every try earns a star, and nothing is marked wrong.
    Not answering during his turn
    With the microphone on, the app listens during his turn. If it hears nothing, it shows the sound again slowly (once or twice, you choose) and never says "wrong". After that it says that is okay and offers another sound.
    Mixing up "I" and "you"
    Requests always use the same frame: I want cookie. Say it the same way each time.
    Sounds, movement, and surprises overwhelm
    Quiet chimes, a volume control, gentle mode with no pulsing, a break button on every screen, and a timer that ends the session before he is tired.
    Motivation changes day to day
    First and Then on the home screen, a choice of activity, stars for trying, and a bubble break.

    Tips for a good session

    Sit beside him. Say each sound yourself too. The app voice says an example word, and your voice is the best model.

    Keep it to 5 to 10 minutes and stop after a success. Accept any try: a breath, a lip movement, a sound that is close. Give the real item right away in I want. Touch only if he is comfortable with it.

    If he is not using words yet, or speech is hard to understand, ask for an SLP evaluation. An SLP can check for apraxia, set goals, and add picture or device-based communication (AAC). Research has not found that AAC holds back speech.

    Settings

    The microphone only measures how loud sound is. Nothing is recorded, saved, or sent. It can tell that he made a sound, not whether the sound was right. A silent mouth try still counts when you tap I tried!
